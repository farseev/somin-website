QA for AI marketing content means checking every asset against six things before it ships: facts, numbers traceable to a source, brand voice, claims and legal, localisation and accessibility. Treat each post, ad or email like a release candidate: run the same checklist every time, record what failed, and give a named person the final sign-off, because the tool never carries the responsibility.

Key takeaways

  • AI drafts fail in predictable places: invented details, numbers with no source, drifting voice, risky claims, literal translation and missing alt text.
  • Every number in a published asset should trace to a post, study, report or ID that a reviewer can open in under a minute.
  • Advertising rules apply however an ad is made, and the UK's ASA says marketers stay responsible for their ads.
  • Localisation is a quality check, not a translation step: CSA Research found 76% of online shoppers prefer to buy with product information in their own language.
  • Automate the mechanical checks and keep a person on meaning, claims and final sign-off.

Why does AI-generated content need its own QA?

Because AI copy fails differently from human copy: it reads fluently while being wrong. A review that asks "does this read well?" passes exactly the errors that matter.

Speed is not the problem; unchecked speed is.

How do you check facts and numbers in AI copy?

Make traceability a rule: no number ships unless someone can open its source in under a minute. Then look specifically for anything the model added that was not in the brief.

  1. Diff against the brief. List every name, number and claim in the draft and tick each one off against the brief or a source. Anything new is a defect until proven otherwise.
  2. Ask for sources in the output. Have the model attach a source link or ID to each figure, and reject drafts that cannot.
  3. Check rounding and dates. "Nearly half" from a 2020 survey is not this year's number.

Grounding helps but does not remove the check. What do advertising rules say about AI-generated content?

The rules do not change because a model wrote the ad. The advertiser carries the same responsibility for claims, endorsements and harm.

The UK's Advertising Standards Authority put it plainly in May 2025: existing rules apply "regardless of how content is generated, edited, or targeted". It adds that disclosure alone is very unlikely to fix a fundamentally misleading message, and that an AI image of a cosmetic effect that does not reflect real-world results is likely to be materially misleading. A follow-up piece of ASA advice for marketers using AI warns against implied endorsements that do not exist and ends with a line every QA lead should pin up: "marketers are ultimately responsible for their ads."

Codes differ by market, so check the local rules wherever an asset runs. For web content, Google's helpful-content guidance asks a similar question from the reader's side: whether the use of automation, including AI generation, is self-evident to visitors through disclosures or in other ways.

How do you QA localisation and accessibility?

For localisation, a native reviewer in each market checks meaning against local audience data, not just words. For accessibility, check text alternatives, captions and contrast before anything publishes.

The stakes for localisation are commercial. CSA Research surveyed 8,709 consumers in 29 countries and found that 76% of online shoppers prefer to buy products with information in their native language, and 40% will never buy from websites in other languages.

Localisation also means fitting the audience, not only the language. In the Keeta HK case study, AI-generated ad lines in English and Cantonese were checked against GWI data for Hong Kong food orderers. That audience under-indexed on budget-driven behaviour, so lines such as "kind to your wallet" were rewritten around curation and quality. A literal translation would have passed a language check and failed the audience.

For accessibility, the W3C's WCAG success criterion 1.1.1 requires that non-text content has a text alternative serving the equivalent purpose. Image generators do not write that alternative for you, and an auto-caption describing "a person smiling" rarely serves the purpose of a product image.

Which checks can you automate, and which need a person?

Automate the mechanical checks; keep a person on meaning. A second model that reviews the first shares many of its blind spots, so it can flag problems but should not approve.

  • Automate: link and UTM validation, banned-word and spelling-variant scans, character limits, alt-text presence, and a check that every figure carries a source tag.
  • Keep human: facts in context, claims and legal review, cultural fit, disclosure decisions and the final sign-off.
  • Log every failure. Tag each defect by checklist row. After a month, the log tells you which prompt, data source or template to fix, the way a regression suite points to the failing module.

FAQ

What is QA for AI marketing content?

It is a repeatable review that checks AI-generated posts, ads and emails for factual accuracy, traceable numbers, brand voice, legal risk, localisation and accessibility before they are published, with a named person signing off.

Do I have to disclose AI-generated content in ads?

In the UK there is no blanket legal requirement, but the ASA says you should disclose AI use where the audience would otherwise be misled. Disclosure does not fix an ad that is misleading in itself.

How do I check statistics written by AI?

Require a source link or ID for every figure, open each source, and confirm the number, the date and the population it describes. Delete any figure you cannot trace.

Can AI check its own marketing content?

It can run mechanical checks and flag likely problems, but a model reviewing a model shares many of the same blind spots. Keep a person on claims, cultural fit and the final approval.
