Google's Target ROAS and Meta's smart bidding both use machine learning to set a bid in every auction, but they optimise for different things. Google's Target ROAS predicts the value of each potential conversion from your campaign history and auction-time signals, then bids to reach the return on ad spend you set. Meta's system prioritises ads by predicted engagement and conversion rates, drawing on the user data it holds across Facebook and Instagram.

Key takeaways

  • Both systems bid automatically at auction time, so neither needs a manual bid per keyword or audience.
  • Google's Target ROAS optimises for conversion value and needs tracked conversion values; Google lists at least 15 conversions in the past 30 days for Search and Shopping campaigns.
  • Meta's smart bidding leans on predicted user engagement and its own user data, so it needs enough conversion history and a clear view of the target audience.
  • Target ROAS earns its keep when conversions vary in value; Meta's strength is reaching people in their feeds before they search.
  • Neither runs itself: both need clean conversion data, continuous monitoring and adjustment as the market changes.

Smart bidding algorithms have become central to digital marketing. They optimise ad spending, improve campaign performance and streamline decision-making. This article compares two of the leading smart bidding solutions: Google's Target Return on Ad Spend (ROAS) and Meta's smart bidding algorithm.

What is smart bidding?

Smart bidding is automated bidding that uses machine learning to set a bid for each individual auction, instead of one manual bid that applies to a whole keyword, placement or audience. The system estimates how likely this impression is to lead to the result you want and what that result is worth, then bids accordingly. Because the estimate is made at auction time, it can weigh signals no person could process in real time, such as the device, location, time of day and, on search, the query itself.

Google groups its automated strategies under the Smart Bidding name: Target CPA, Target ROAS, Maximize conversions and Maximize conversion value. Meta offers comparable automated bid strategies in Ads Manager, which this article calls "Meta's smart bidding" as a group.

How does Google's Target ROAS work?

Google's Target ROAS shows what machine learning can do in bidding. It aims to maximise conversion value while reaching a specific return on ad spend that you set.

What are its key features?

  • Data-driven decisions: it uses historical campaign data and contextual auction-time signals.

  • Real-time bid adjustments: it tailors the bid for each auction to the likelihood of a high-value conversion.

  • Maximising conversion value: it focuses on the quality of conversions rather than their quantity.

  • Flexibility and control: advertisers can set different ROAS targets for different campaigns.

  • Performance insights: it offers detailed reports on how the strategy performs.

Target ROAS has entry requirements. Google's help page lists at least 15 conversions in the past 30 days for Search and Shopping campaigns, with different thresholds for other campaign types, and you must assign values to the conversions you track before you apply it. Google also suggests reporting conversion values for four weeks, or for one to two conversion cycles if that is longer, before you set a target. Once it is running, it needs continuous monitoring to adapt to market changes.

The target itself is a lever. A higher ROAS target makes the system bid more cautiously, which can protect margin but reduce conversion volume; a lower target lets it compete in more auctions. Google also notes that performance generally improves with fewer, larger campaigns that collect more conversions, because the model has more data to learn from.

How does Meta's smart bidding algorithm work?

Meta's smart bidding algorithm, which runs across platforms such as Facebook and Instagram, also relies on advanced machine learning.

What are its key features?

  • User engagement focus: it prioritises ads based on predicted user engagement and conversion rates.

  • Auction-time bidding: it adjusts bids in real time, much like Google's approach.

  • Comprehensive use of user data: it draws on Meta's extensive user data for targeting.

Meta's auction does not reward the highest bid alone. It weighs the advertiser's bid together with the estimated chance that this person will take the action the ad set is optimised for and an assessment of ad quality. An ad the model expects people to respond to can win an auction at a lower bid than a competitor's, which is why creative and audience fit show up directly in your costs.

Meta's algorithm needs sufficient historical data to optimise and a clear understanding of the target audience. New ad sets, and ad sets that receive significant edits, go through a learning phase in which delivery is less stable while the system gathers data. Conversion signals come from the Meta pixel and the Conversions API, so gaps in tracking leave the model guessing.

How do Google and Meta smart bidding compare?

The comparison shows several contrasts and similarities:

  • Use of machine learning: both use sophisticated machine learning, but Google emphasises conversion value while Meta focuses on user engagement.

  • Real-time adjustments: both adjust bids dynamically, but they differ in their data focus. Google analyses historical campaign data, whereas Meta draws on its vast user data.

  • Flexibility: both provide adjustable parameters, but their effectiveness varies across campaign types and objectives.

  • User interface and integration: Google's platform is often seen as more user-friendly, while Meta's system offers deeper integration with social media marketing.

What do case studies show?

Various case studies illustrate what these algorithms can do. Search Engine Land, citing Google, reports that advertisers who upgraded from Smart Shopping to Maximize conversion value with Target ROAS saw as much as a 30% uplift in conversion value, while a fashion retailer saw improved engagement rates on Meta's platform.

Treat any headline figure as a hypothesis for your own account. Both platforms offer built-in experiments, so you can run an automated strategy against your current approach and compare the results before you move all of your spend.

What are the challenges and limitations?

Advertisers often face data privacy concerns, especially with Meta's dependence on user data. Privacy changes such as Apple's App Tracking Transparency framework, introduced in 2021, reduced the signals ad platforms receive from apps, which makes first-party data and server-side tracking more important. Both platforms require continuous monitoring and adjustment to maintain performance.

Both models are also opaque, so you see what they bid but not why, and both are only as good as the conversion data you feed them: duplicated, missing or wrongly valued conversions teach them the wrong lesson.

What comes next for smart bidding?

Future trends point to even more personalised ad targeting and more efficient machine learning. The growing importance of data privacy and the evolution of AI technology are likely to shape these algorithms further.

Google's Target ROAS and Meta's smart bidding algorithm each offer distinct advantages and challenges, and their effectiveness depends on the needs and goals of each campaign. As digital marketing advances, these tools will become more sophisticated, offering more precise targeting and higher returns on investment. Because Meta's auction rewards ads people respond to, the creative you feed it matters as much as the bid; that is why SOMIN lets you score ads before you spend.

FAQ

Does Google's Target ROAS need a minimum number of conversions?

Yes. Google's help page lists at least 15 conversions in the past 30 days for Search and Shopping campaigns, with different thresholds for other campaign types. You also need conversion values set up for the conversions you track.

What is the main difference between Google and Meta smart bidding?

Google's Target ROAS focuses on conversion value and relies on historical campaign data and auction-time signals. Meta's smart bidding focuses on predicted user engagement and conversion rates and relies on Meta's own user data.

Why does Meta's smart bidding need historical data?

The algorithm predicts who is likely to engage or convert, and it needs past results to make those predictions. New or heavily edited ad sets go through a learning phase while it gathers that data.

Can I leave smart bidding to run on its own?

No. Both platforms need continuous monitoring and adjustment, clean conversion tracking and fresh creative to keep performing as the market changes.
