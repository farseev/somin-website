Agents should assist on judgement-heavy work such as personas, copy and creative scoring. What is an agentic workflow?

An agentic workflow is a process in which a language model does part of the work by calling tools and, in places, choosing its own next step. It sits between fixed automation and a fully autonomous agent, and most agency value lives in that middle ground.

Anthropic's engineering team draws the distinction cleanly in Building effective agents. Workflows orchestrate models and tools through predefined code paths; agents dynamically direct their own process and tool use. Its advice is to find the simplest solution that works and add complexity only when needed, and it describes agents that pause for human feedback at checkpoints and stop after a set number of iterations.

For an agency that translates into a design rule. Use a fixed workflow where the steps are known, such as a weekly report. Use an agent where the path is open, such as researching a category the team has never pitched. Put a checkpoint wherever a client or a budget is affected.

Why do so many agent projects stall?

Because teams automate the impressive, judgement-heavy work first, instead of the dull work that is easy to check. The demo looks good, the output needs heavy correction, and the business case disappears.

Gartner predicts that over 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by the end of 2027, citing escalating costs, unclear business value or inadequate risk controls. Its advice maps neatly onto three modes: use agents when decisions are needed, automation for routine workflows and assistants for simple retrieval.

McKinsey's State of AI in 2026, a survey of 1,719 participants, finds about two in ten respondents scaling AI agents across their organisation. Among its high performers, nearly three-quarters report fundamentally redesigning workflows because of AI, against one-quarter of everyone else.

How should agencies decide what to automate first?

Score each task on volume, reversibility and verifiability. Automate when all three are high, assist when the output needs judgement to verify, and keep it human when a mistake cannot be undone or the client relationship is at stake.

Where should agents assist rather than decide?

On work where most of the effort is research and the final step is taste: personas, tensions, concepts and copy. The agent proposes with evidence; a person chooses.

What must stay human, and how do you enforce it?

Anything that spends money, makes a public claim or carries accountability to the client. Enforce it in the workflow itself, not in a policy document.

  • Approval steps in the tool. Spend and publish actions wait for a named person, by default.
  • Stopping conditions. Cap iterations and cost per run, so a confused agent stops instead of looping.
  • Read-only by default. Give agents write access one tool at a time, as described in our guide to connecting Claude to marketing data with MCP.
  • Logs a person can read. Every tool call and approval should be visible after the fact.

FAQ

What are agentic workflows for agencies?

They are agency processes in which AI agents gather data, draft outputs or take steps through connected tools, with people approving the decisions that matter. Reporting, monitoring and research are the usual starting points.

Which agency tasks should be automated first?

Tasks that repeat often, can be checked against a source and are cheap to correct: cross-platform data pulls, competitor monitoring and first drafts of client reports.

Will AI agents replace agency strategists?

They replace hours, not judgement. Agents compress research and drafting, while the recommendation, the client relationship and accountability for claims stay with people.

How do you keep a human in the loop with AI agents?

Build approval steps into the tool for anything that spends money or publishes, cap how long an agent can run, start with read-only access and keep a log of every action.
