Shadow AI is the use of AI tools at work without the organisation's knowledge or approval. Shadow AI governance is the set of rules and checks that bring that use into the open. For a marketing team, a one-page policy is enough to start: approved tools, data classes, human review, logging and an incident route.

Key takeaways

  • Shadow AI is normal behaviour from people trying to work faster; ban-only policies tend to push it out of sight.
  • The main risks are data leakage, unchecked claims, copyright and brand-voice drift, and unlogged commitments.
  • A good policy fits on one page and answers five questions: which tools, which data, who reviews, what is logged, and what to do when something goes wrong.
  • Pair the policy with an approved tool list that is actually good enough to use.
  • Review the page every quarter; tools and rules change fast.

What is shadow AI?

It is any AI tool, plug-in, browser extension or agent that staff use for work without it being reviewed by IT, legal or the marketing lead. A copywriter pasting a client brief into a free chatbot is the classic case. So is a freelancer using an image generator that the contract does not mention.

The cause is usually not bad intent. People adopt the tool that helps. The same pressure drives AI tool sprawl, which is a cost problem as well as a risk one.

Why does shadow AI matter for marketing?

Marketing handles customer data, unreleased plans, client confidential material and public claims. Each can go wrong through unapproved AI.

  • Data leakage. Confidential or personal data entered into a tool whose terms allow it to be kept or used for training.
  • False claims. Models invent statistics, quotes and prices. See AI hallucinations in marketing.
  • Rights and disclosure. Unclear ownership of generated assets, and missing disclosure where rules require it.
  • Brand drift. Many people prompting differently produce content that sounds like nobody.
  • Hidden commitments. Agents that reply to customers or publish without a record. Our piece on the shadow ledger covers this.

Is there a recognised framework to build on?

Yes. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) published its AI Risk Management Framework on 26 January 2023 as a voluntary resource. NIST also released a Generative AI Profile (NIST-AI-600-1) on 26 July 2024 to help organisations identify risks specific to generative AI. The framework is written for organisations that build or deploy AI, so a marketing team should borrow its ideas — govern, map, measure and manage — rather than adopt it whole. NIST's page notes that the framework is being revised, so check the current version.

Your legal, security or data-protection lead may also need to apply local rules. This article is not legal advice.

What should the one-page policy say?

Use these six sections. Keep each to a few lines.

  1. Purpose and scope. Who it covers: employees, freelancers and agencies working on your brand.
  2. Approved tools. A named list, with an owner and a way to request a new tool. Anything off the list needs approval first.
  3. Data classes. Say what may go into which tool.
    • Public (published content): allowed in approved tools.
    • Internal (plans, drafts): approved tools with business terms only.
    • Confidential (client data, unreleased launches, contracts): only tools approved for it.
    • Personal data (customer names, emails, audience lists): not in AI tools unless privacy has signed off.
  4. Human review. A named person checks facts, numbers, quotes, claims, brand voice and legal wording before anything ships. Use our QA checklist for AI content.
  5. Logging. Record the tool, the date, the purpose and the reviewer for published AI-assisted work. Agents that act on their own need an audit trail and an approval gate.
  6. Incidents. If sensitive data was pasted into an unapproved tool, or a false claim went live: tell the named contact within a set time, do not delete evidence, and expect no blame for early reporting.

How do you bring existing shadow use into the open?

  1. Ask first. Run a short, no-blame survey: which AI tools do you use, for what?
  2. Sort the answers into approve, replace and stop.
  3. Offer a good approved option for each common task, or the list will be ignored.
  4. Train briefly. One hour on the policy and on checking AI output. See how to train junior marketers on AI.
  5. Review quarterly and update the tool list.

FAQ

Is shadow AI the same as shadow IT?

It is a form of it. Shadow IT is any unapproved software; shadow AI is the AI-specific case, with added risks from data being used for training and from content that looks right but is not.

Should we ban AI tools?

A blanket ban is hard to enforce and usually pushes use out of sight. A clear approved list, plus data rules, gives people a safe route to the same benefit.

Who should own the AI policy in a marketing team?

The marketing lead should own it day to day, with input from legal, security and data protection. Name one person so questions have a destination.

Do freelancers and agencies need to follow it?

Yes. Put the policy into contracts or briefs, and ask partners which AI tools they use on your work and what data they enter.

How often should we update the policy?

Review it every quarter and whenever a new tool, law or incident changes the picture. A stale page is worse than none because it gives false comfort.
