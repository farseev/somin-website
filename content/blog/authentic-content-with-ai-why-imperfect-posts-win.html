Authentic content with AI is possible when AI handles research, drafting and scheduling, and people supply what audiences respond to: real stories, specific details, visible imperfection and a recognisable voice. Polished, generic posts are now the default output of every tool, so the slightly raw, human post is the one that stands out and earns engagement.

Key takeaways

  • In SOMIN's social-listening data, raw and real stories outperform polished, perfect posts.
  • Audiences engage when content feels chosen by a person; automation that removes the person makes interactions transactional.
  • Low-quality AI content is saturating feeds, which raises the value of anything visibly human.
  • The practical answer is a division of labour: AI for listening, drafting and scale; people for stories, voice and replies.

Why does imperfect content outperform polished posts?

Because perfection now reads as manufactured. When anyone can generate a flawless image and a smooth caption in seconds, polish stops signalling care and starts signalling a template.

SOMIN's social-listening workspace captures this in the insight "Authenticity thrives in the cracks of perfection." Its angle, the desire for authenticity, draws on Self-Determination Theory, which holds that people are motivated by autonomy, competence and relatedness. A raw, real story offers relatedness that a polished post does not. The insight rests on 330 engagements across 24 posts, and the persona behind it, the Dynamic Digital Strategist, sits on the agency side, where output is expected to be both polished and frequent.

Instagram's head has reached a similar view from the platform side. In a year-end essay on Threads, reported by Tom's Guide in January 2026, Adam Mosseri argued that a perfectly lit photo no longer proves anything happened, and that younger users increasingly share unflattering candids in private chats and stories.

What happens when automation replaces the human voice?

Interactions turn transactional, and audiences notice. Automation works for logistics; it fails when it stands in for the relationship.

A second insight from the same workspace, "Authenticity thrives when technology takes a backseat to human connection", frames this through Symbolic Interactionism: meaning is created in the exchange between people, so a post or reply carries weight because someone chose it. When automation removes that choice, the interaction risks feeling soulless. This insight rests on 488 engagements across 36 posts.

SOMIN's moments-of-truth model shows marketers feel the tension themselves. For a persona working through AI-driven marketing, it surfaces the thought "I need to streamline our operations while keeping the human touch in our customer interactions." For another, the question is simply "Can AI actually help me be more creative and efficient?"

Why is AI content saturation making authenticity scarcer?

Because feeds are filling with content nobody cared about making. Merriam-Webster chose "slop" as its 2025 word of the year, defined as digital content of low quality produced usually in quantity by means of artificial intelligence.

SOMIN's creative-content workspace, which tracks AI content tools, sees the same pressure from the marketer's side. A study of 108 posts (September to October 2025) on AI in brand visibility and content creation describes teams pushed to produce content at unprecedented pace, worries about authenticity, and a saturation of low-quality AI-generated content that makes authentic connection harder. A separate theme on marketing in the Philippines (57 posts) names the pressure to deliver quality content at scale without losing authenticity.

Audiences say they want to know what is real. Getty Images' Building Trust in the Age of AI research, covering more than 30,000 adults in 25 countries, found 98% agree that authentic images and videos are pivotal to establishing trust, and almost 90% want to know whether an image was created with AI.

How do AI teams keep content human?

By deciding, task by task, what the machine does and what a person does. A workable split looks like this.

ListeningTrack what is landing in the category and which tensions recurDecide which conversations the brand has a right to join
AnglePropose angles tied to real tensionsPick the one with a true story behind it
First draftStructure, variants and captionsAdd the detail only you know: a name, a number, a place, a mistake

VisualsConcepts, backgrounds and format variantsReal photos and phone footage for people, products and places

PublishingSchedule and post across channelsApprove anything sensitive, timely or claim-bearing

Five habits that keep AI-assisted content human

  1. Start from a real conversation, not a prompt. Open each post with something your audience said, asked or complained about.
  2. Keep one human detail per post. A name, a behind-the-scenes moment, a number from your own work.
  3. Let some rough edges through. Phone footage, an unscripted take, a draft that shows the thinking.
  4. Edit out the tells. Generic openers, tidy lists of three and adjectives that could describe any brand.
  5. Disclose and check where it matters. Label AI-made people or images if the audience could be misled, and run factual claims through a pre-publish check.

SOMIN's Social Media Manager is built around this split. It watches what is landing in the category, writes in the account's own voice and can run with a sign-off checkpoint, so a person decides what goes out.

What does this mean for creator programmes?

Creators are the authenticity layer most brands already have. The mistake is briefing them so tightly that their content sounds like the brand's AI drafts.

Brief creators on a territory, not a script. Give each one a lane they can credibly own, the tension it answers and the product truth behind it, then let them say it their way. SOMIN's creator workflow builds briefs like this, in each creator's own voice, and reports 2.4 times higher GMV when a creator owns her lane.

The ads-optimisation workspace adds a warning for brands that skip this step. Its insight "Authenticity is the antidote to homogenization" links AI-generated content to templated, look-alike messaging that leaves brands without a voice of their own.

FAQ

Can AI-generated content be authentic?

Yes, when a person supplies the substance: a real story, a specific detail and a voice the audience recognises. AI is good at structure and scale; authenticity comes from what only the brand or creator knows.

Why do imperfect posts get more engagement?

Polish has become cheap, so it no longer signals effort. In SOMIN's social-listening data, raw, real stories outperform polished, perfect posts, which the insight links to people's need for connection.

Should brands disclose AI-generated content?

Disclose when the audience could be misled without it, especially for realistic people, endorsements or product results. In the UK, the ASA applies exactly this test rather than a blanket labelling rule.

How do I keep my brand voice when using AI?

Give the tool your tone guide and real examples, then edit every draft against them. Read posts aloud and cut any line nobody on the team would say.

Pull last month's five best and five worst posts and sort them by how polished they were. If the raw ones won, plan next month around more of them: start each post from a real conversation, keep one human detail, and let AI take research and scheduling off your plate so the team has time to do it.
