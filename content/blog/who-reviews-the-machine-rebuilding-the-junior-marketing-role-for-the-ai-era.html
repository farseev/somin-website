Ask a marketing team what happens when AI writes every first draft, and most will talk about speed. Few will talk about the person who used to learn from that draft. That omission sits at the center of a recent Gartner prediction, reported by Marketing Week, which suggests that by 2030 most high performing marketing teams will be able to remove the traditional entry level role. The prediction is easy to accept at face value. It is also easy to misread.

What the Gartner Data Actually Shows

The headline figure is 18 per cent. In a survey of roughly 1,300 senior leaders, that share said they had already eliminated roles because automation now handles the work. The supporting figures got less attention. Sixteen per cent of the same leaders had created new roles, and close to a third had redesigned existing ones. Gartner's own warning is the part worth keeping: leaders risk confusing the automation of junior tasks with no longer needing junior people.

Read together, the numbers describe a profession in the middle of reshaping its bottom rung, not abandoning it.

The Apprenticeship Hidden Inside the Task List

We tend to define entry level jobs by what they produce: the competitor deck, the draft caption, the weekly report, the media plan. AI can now produce each of these, so the job appears to vanish.

That view mistakes the vehicle for the destination. A junior built the deck so that a senior could challenge it. In that exchange the junior learned what a strong insight looks like, why a claim needs evidence and when a number is misleading. The deliverable was incidental, and the correction was the education.

Automate the deliverable and the correction disappears with it, unless someone deliberately designs a replacement. Most teams have not.

Audience research points the same way. Across public conversations among marketers over the past two months, one tension keeps recurring. Interns and junior staff describe feeling undervalued and disconnected. They worry about leaning too heavily on AI. They ask for meaningful work that builds real skills. None of this is a request to be shielded from automation. It is a request for a career path that still teaches something.

A Tuesday Night, and a Quarter of Weak Content

Consider a second year marketing executive at 11pm, typing into a search bar a question like: how can AI make my work better without replacing my job? Our data shows this search intent clearly among junior audiences, and it is a sensible thing to ask.

That same week, her CMO is reviewing a quarter of AI generated content that underperformed. The pattern will be familiar to many leaders. The content arrived quickly, still required heavy human revision and was assessed with measurement frameworks built for another era. Budget flowed to tools that nobody examined closely enough. Having invested in the programme, leadership defends it, tweaks it and searches for the one chart that looks healthy.

The person best placed to say the programme is not working is often the junior closest to the output, who sees every draft before it is polished. She is also the person whose role sits on the reduction list.

This is the trap. The most valuable skill in an AI workflow is the ability to look at machine produced work and explain precisely why it is wrong. Teams are cutting the very role where that skill is formed.

From Making to Checking: Three Practical Shifts

Gartner's guidance is to redesign the junior job rather than preserve it as it was. In practice that means three moves.

  1. Hire for judgement. Evaluate candidates on whether they can assess and improve AI output, not on how quickly they can produce a first draft.

  2. Make the audit the first assignment. From day one, have juniors review AI outputs against evidence. Testing claims, sources and audience fit is genuine work, and it is the kind that compounds over a career.

  3. Teach decisions sooner. Mentor juniors on business reasoning, meaning why one option beat another, instead of on tool shortcuts that will be obsolete within six months.

Marketing Week's own career and salary survey shows the stakes. It found that 66.5 per cent of respondents saw an AI skills gap in their team over the past year, and 73.5 per cent of CMOs and marketing directors are worried about AI capability. Leaders are anxious about a gap that cutting the bottom rung will only widen.

Why Southeast Asia Will Feel It First

Teams across the region are lean by default. Independent agencies compete with regional networks that have many times their headcount, and in house teams in Jakarta, Manila and Ho Chi Minh City manage calendars across several markets with only a handful of people. When automation removes a junior seat, the saving appears in this quarter's results, while the cost arrives roughly three years later.

Shared insight platforms have helped smaller agencies reach a depth of analysis that once required a much larger team. That advantage holds only if someone can question what the platform says. The same applies as discovery shifts toward AI answers. When a chatbot summarises your category, a person has to read that summary critically and spot where it misrepresents you.

A region whose digital economy runs on young talent cannot afford to stop developing its own strategists. Next year's graduates from NUS, UI and Ateneo are not a cost line. They are the planning bench of 2030, provided someone trains them.

Evidence That Can Be Challenged

A redesigned junior role only works if AI output can actually be checked. An opaque answer leaves a junior with two choices: accept it or redo the work by hand. Neither builds judgement.

Every conclusion from an AI research tool should trace back to the published posts behind it. A persona, a tension or a recommended angle can then be opened, read and debated, which turns review into a real task rather than a rubber stamp. The real benefit is not the hours saved. It is that those hours shift from gathering material to interpreting it.

The Question to Take Into the Next Headcount Meeting

Juniors learn judgement by hearing seniors reason aloud, in reviews, in briefs and through leaders who explain how they decide as well as what they decided. That only works if a junior is still in the room.

So before the next headcount conversation, ask one thing: if AI writes every first draft, who on your team is learning to tell a good one from a bad one?
